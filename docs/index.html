01Question

Using only the lap times a team has seen so far, can a model tell when a Formula 1 driver is about to pit, within ±2 laps of the actual stop, better than a tyre-age rule and a logistic-regression classifier?

Teams pit mostly because tyres wear out, and worn tyres make lap times slower. But tyre condition is not public. So we treat it as a hidden state and infer it from lap times with a hidden Markov model (HMM) that we wrote from scratch.

After every lap, each model decides whether to raise an alarm ("this driver will pit now"). It may only use laps that have already happened. An alarm counts as correct if a real pit stop happens within ±2 laps of it.

What we claim, and what we don't

  • Pit stops are only a proxy for tyre wear. Teams also pit for strategy (undercuts, safety cars, traffic), so some stops cannot be predicted from lap times at all.
  • This is prediction, not causation.
  • A useful answer is an honest comparison with simple baselines on a season the models never saw, plus a look at where they fail. A modest or negative result is still an answer.

Terms used below: a stint is the run of laps between two pit stops (or between the last stop and the finish). An alarm is a model's call to pit on a given lap.

03Preprocessing

Cleaning

  • Drop the first lap of each race (standing start, not representative).
  • Drop pit-in and pit-out laps (time lost in the pit lane, not on tyres).
  • Drop laps slower than 1.35× the driver's median lap in that race (safety cars, incidents, traffic).
  • Stints are inferred from pit-stop laps, since Ergast has no stint column.

Known issue: the median in the 1.35× rule is taken over the whole race, so it uses a little information from later laps. It only decides which laps are dropped, not any feature value, but a fully causal version would use the median so far.

Features per lap

Each feature uses only the current lap and earlier laps in the same stint:

  1. Lap-time delta — lap time minus the best lap so far in this stint.
  2. Rolling degradation — 3-lap rolling mean of the delta.
  3. Tyre age — laps kept so far in the stint ÷ 40.

04Baselines and methods

Tyre-age rule (baseline)

One alarm per stint, when tyre age passes a threshold. Threshold picked on 2019 from 0.60–0.95 (24–38 laps). Asks: is counting laps enough?

Logistic regression (baseline)

Per-lap classifier on the three features, labelled "pit within the next 3 laps", with class weights for the rare positives. An alarm is raised at the start of each run of positive laps, so it can raise several per stint.

HMM (main method)

Gaussian HMM written from scratch (scaled forward–backward, Viterbi, Baum–Welch). Trained without pit labels. After each lap, forward filtering gives P(worn | laps so far). One alarm per stint when this passes a threshold (0.3–0.9, picked on 2019).

HMM without tyre age (ablation)

Same HMM, but tyre age removed from its inputs. If it does as well as the full HMM, the lap times carry the signal; if it collapses, the HMM was mostly counting laps.

How the HMM is set up

  • Left-to-right transitions: tyres never recover, so the model can stay fresh, move to worn, or stay worn, but never go back.
  • Starts fresh: π = [1, 0]. Each stint is a new sequence.
  • Gaussian emissions: each state has its own mean and spread for the lap features.
  • Filtering, not Viterbi: Viterbi looks at the whole stint, including the future. Forward filtering uses only laps up to now, which is what a pit wall actually has.
  • Two states, chosen on 2019: we tried 2, 3 and 4 states. The best 2019 F1 was 0.091 with 2 states, against 0.089 with 4 and 0.058 with 3, so the final model has no separate "degrading" state. Both the full HMM and the ablation chose 2 states.

Why an HMM? There are no labels for tyre condition, laps within a stint depend on each other, and the learned states and transitions can be read and checked.

Two checks from the proposal were not done: a Gaussian mixture model (an HMM with no time component) and a comparison of our HMM against hmmlearn. See Limitations. The third, alarms before vs after the pit, is in Errors.

05Evaluation design

  • Matching: within each driver-race, alarms are matched one-to-one to real pit stops. An alarm is a hit if its pit is within ±2 laps. Extra alarms are false positives; unmatched pits are misses.
  • Pooled metrics: precision, recall and F1 are computed from TP/FP/FN summed over all driver-races, not averaged per driver (so drivers with one stop don't count as much as a whole race).
  • Sensitivity: results also at ±1 and ±3 laps.
  • Timing: the distribution of alarm lap − pit lap shows whether models are early or late.
  • Not accuracy: pits are about 1 lap in 28, so "never pit" would score over 96% accuracy.
  • Tuning is separate from testing: all choices use 2019; 2020 is used once.

What the numbers say

The HMM does not beat the simple tyre-age rule. At ±2 laps the four models are within 0.022 F1 of each other: tyre-age rule 0.107, HMM 0.106, HMM without tyre age 0.101, logistic regression 0.085. Every model catches fewer than 1 stop in 10 (the HMM finds 46 of 515). At ±1 lap all are between 0.043 and 0.062. Only at ±3 laps does the HMM pull ahead (0.180 against 0.137 for the tyre-age rule), which says its alarms tend to land a few laps before the stop rather than on it.

The HMM is reading lap times, not just counting laps. Removing tyre age from its inputs costs only 0.005 F1 at ±2 (0.106 → 0.101) and 0.014 at ±3. The ablation still does almost as well as the tyre-age rule, which uses nothing but lap counts. But the lap-time signal is weak: it is about as informative as a lap counter, not more.

Best races for the HMM: Italy (11 of 35 stops caught) and Belgium (4 of 20). Worst: the 70th Anniversary and Russian GPs (none caught).

08Errors: when and why models miss

Are alarms early or late?

For every alarm paired with a pit in the same driver-race: alarm lap − pit lap. Negative = early (useful to a team), positive = after the stop had already happened.

Caveat: the timing file pairs each alarm with the nearest unused pit in that driver-race, with no distance limit and not restricted to the same stint. So far-away pairs (the outer bars) are often an alarm in one stint paired with a pit from another, not a real near-miss.

Final-stint alarms dominate the false alarms, and stops that have nothing to do with gradual wear (short stints, field-wide stops) make up most of the identifiable misses. We cannot split the rest into undercuts or wet races without track-status and weather data.

09Limitations and what went wrong

A bug made our first test results invalid

On 28 October 2026 we found that a pandas merge reset the row index, so tyre age was read from the wrong laps for about 96% of rows. At the start of each new stint the feature still carried the old stint's high value, so models raised alarms 1–2 laps after a pit. The symmetric ±2 window counted these as correct. About three quarters of logistic regression's correct alarms came from this.

We fixed it by counting kept laps within each stint, then re-ran tuning, training and the 2020 test from scratch. The first 2020 test was invalid. No setting was chosen by looking at 2020 results, before or after the fix.

Lesson: a symmetric tolerance window rewards alarms that come after the event. That is why we also report the timing of alarms (see Errors).

Other limitations

  • No tyre compounds. Softs and hards wear at very different rates; one model and one "40-lap" scale must cover both.
  • No safety-car or weather flags. Many stops happen because a safety car makes them cheap, not because tyres are worn. We can't remove those laps or races cleanly.
  • Pit laps are a noisy label. Strategy (undercuts, covering a rival, traffic) moves stops by several laps.
  • One test season. 2020 was unusual (COVID calendar, repeat circuits, 17 races), so results may not carry over to other seasons.
  • Unequal chances. Logistic regression can raise several alarms per stint; the others raise at most one.
  • Cleaning median uses the whole race (see Preprocessing).
  • Timing pairs are not limited to the same stint (see the caveat under Errors).
  • Two proposal checks not done: no Gaussian mixture model comparison and no check of our HMM against hmmlearn.
  • Tyre-age threshold at the edge of the grid. 2019 picked 0.60, the lowest value tried, so a lower threshold might have done better.

10Conclusion

No. Using lap times alone, none of the models predicts the pit lap well, and the HMM does no better than counting laps: F1 = 0.106 at ±2 against 0.107 for the tyre-age rule and 0.085 for logistic regression.

The reasons show up in the errors. Many stops are not caused by gradual wear: whole-field stops under interruptions, early stops after short stints, and strategic calls. Meanwhile, the most worn tyres of the race, in the final stint, are never changed. Without tyre compounds and track status, a model sees the same lap-time pattern in all of these cases. The most likely improvements are compound and safety-car data, and predicting a window of laps (the HMM is clearly better at ±3) instead of an exact lap.

What we take away: an unsupervised HMM gives an interpretable, causal estimate of tyre state, but the pit lap depends on much more than tyre state, and a symmetric tolerance window can hide models that only react after the fact.
